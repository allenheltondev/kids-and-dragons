## Lore

> *A stone without a place is only half a story.*

The first burrowfolk settlements grew along the low chalk and clay ridges between the Sunward Fields. A flooded cellar and a dry well can be the same problem viewed from different doors, so their oldest maps join the ground above to the layers below. Children learn those maps by carrying ordinary stones home and explaining where each was found; an elder may return a dazzling specimen and keep a dull one that finishes a missing layer. A home's stone shelf records its repairs, visitors and mistakes, rather than its wealth.
When gemstones began falling in the north, some burrowfolk went to earn money, some to bring relatives home, and some because stones arriving without their layers troubled them. Their notebooks do not make them wiser than their neighbors. A surveyor can mistake a rumor for a map, and a tired prospector can sell tomorrow's clue for tonight's meal. Ma Ruddle's lost wagon and Dunmore's embarrassed good intentions are individual stories, not the character of a people.

### For Fun

- Pebble exchanges: trade an ordinary stone and the precise story of the place it came from.
- Repair circles: mend a neighbor's tool while the neighbor tells the part of its history they usually leave out.
- Rain-window contests: predict which gutter will drip first, then inspect the one everyone forgot.

### Getting Along

A shared shelf in the village hall holds samples from common ground; private shelves belong to households, and permission matters. Meals accompany difficult corrections so a mistaken map need not become a quarrel. These customs invite cooperation without guaranteeing it.
