# Designing a worthwhile chapter session

Read this for a new chapter, a substantial rewrite, or feedback that a chapter is
boring, repetitive, too short or too long. Keep the requested duration. A normal
campaign sitting aims around thirty minutes unless the brief specifies another
length; do not impose that length on practice chapters or brief travel hubs.

## Design the evening before counting scenes

Write the chapter's goal and required closing beat. Sketch a few story movements
that change what the party knows, wants or can do. Each needs a purpose beyond
another obstacle to the same action. An investigation might move through
preparation, discovery, a complication, then deciding who benefits from the work.
That is one example, not a required act structure.

Make a small time budget including reading aloud, discussing choices, sharing
clues, taking interactive turns and seeing consequences. For a thirty-minute
chapter, a possible split is 7 / 7 / 8 / 8 minutes across four movements. Adjust
the split to the actual adventure. Do not impose artificial waits, require
replays, inflate paragraphs or add chores to fill it. Keep necessary substance
on required routes, not only optional branches. Class abilities can save work
without skipping the chapter's whole middle.

The older pacing model, check percentages and scene totals are diagnostics.
`estimatedMinutes: 30` states intent; it does not validate duration. Label a
budget as planned, and label a family session's timing as observed. Check the
shortest reachable route as well as an engaged route. If gates depend on earlier
choices, walk those choices through the engine; a static graph cannot establish
that the party could take every path it draws.

## Match play to the story

Read the current [interaction authoring guide](../../../../content/README.md#story-interactions)
before choosing controls. Use what exists: shared clue sequences, memory,
tracking, timing/precision, balancing, cooperation, relay and playful
contributions, alongside consequential conversation, exploration, class/species
abilities, inventory, rifts and combat where the story calls for them.

Different fictional jobs should feel different to play. A loading task may suit
balance; finding a clear moment may suit timing; moving something together may
suit cooperation; a pattern recalled from an observation may suit memory.
Avoid turning every discovery into ordering three already-ordered labels or
every physical job into holding a button. Do not force combat or all mechanics
into a chapter to demonstrate features.

For each activity, give one clear cue and an understandable result. Honor solo
props, relaxed play, shared clues, retries and untimed assistance. Story choices
remain untimed; a narrated emergency is not a countdown unless the runtime
explicitly implements one. Do not describe new animations, resource costs or
ability bonuses the engine does not support. Use existing art, cast, music and
rift presentation where they support the moment. Read the cue aloud as to an
eight-year-old. Keep humor in actions and reactions; clarity comes first.

## Make decisions survive their result scenes

For the chapter's important choices, record this mapping while designing:

| Before the choice | Immediate result | Later consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What do players know, and what do they give up? | What changes visibly? | Which reachable task, capability, evidence, cost or ending differs? |

A choice need not be a moral dilemma. Choosing equipment can change a later
interaction. Choosing what to protect can save evidence but require repairing a
route, or preserve the route but lose details from the record. Make the tradeoff
clear enough for children to explain their reasoning before they choose.

Do not merge outcomes into an identical state and pretend they mattered. Use
actual branch destinations, effects, gates or narration variants to show the
changed situation. Check that flags are both written and read. A changed color
of description is flavor; a changed task, resource or available outcome is a
consequence. Keep flavor and free play too, without counting them as major
choices. Do not punish a reasonable choice by inventing an undisclosed cost.

Say whether the consequence is local or persists into another chapter. Inspect
the actual campaign persistence mechanism before promising a callback. Changes
to items, coins and other resources need real effects, not only narration.
Keep an ungated way forward beside class/species and carried-flag options.

## Verify play, then test timing with families

- Validate schemas, reachability, continuity, goal completion and required
  discoveries. Preserve rewards, established campaign facts and protected
  mysteries unless changing them is authorized.
- Exercise the interaction controls through the engine, including assistance
  and relevant misses. A missed action may change a plan or require help;
  an ordinary failed roll should not erase an earned observation.
- Play complete combinations of important decisions, including solo and
  multiplayer and an efficient class-assisted route. Assert that the prepared
  equipment is used, the recovery task changes, losses stay lost, and the
  ending acknowledges the selected priority. Test these outcomes, not a quota
  of scenes, votes, checks or minigames.
- Recheck merges as parties arriving through each different branch. A recovered
  sketch must not regain its lost fine measurements without an actual recovery.
- Regenerate campaign graph proofs after changing graph structure or chapter
  flag writes. Honor pinned-campaign compatibility rules.
- In family testing, time the story movements and note repeated explanations,
  aimless tapping, excluded players, discussion and requests for another turn.
  Adjust the part that needs depth or trimming while preserving the brief's
  session length. Automated correctness is not evidence that a chapter is fun.
